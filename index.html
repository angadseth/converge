    On the Convergence of Students through MLF, MLT and MLP A senior's field guide to the machine learning courses of the IIT Madras BS Diploma in Data Science

    Angad Jangir* BS in Data Science and Applications, IIT Madras github.com/angadseth

    Abstract

    Three courses carry most of the Diploma in Data Science: Machine Learning Foundations (MLF), Machine Learning Techniques (MLT) and Machine Learning Practice (MLP), with the MLP Project run as a Kaggle competition. This guide explains what each course asks of you week by week, how the courses depend on each other, and exactly how each final score is computed, with every formula taken from the official grading document. It then gives a method for each kind of assessment (weekly assignments, quizzes, programming exams, Kaggle assignments, the project vivas and the end term), a marks calculator, the Sep 2026 term on one chart, and every resource I could open and check: the official lecture playlists (about 92 hours across the three courses), the course teams' notes, free textbooks and the best lectures elsewhere.

    There are no answers to graded work here. Every rule cites its source, and when this guide and an official page disagree, the official page wins.

    Keywords: gradient descent, linear algebra, PCA, SVM, scikit-learn, OPPE, Kaggle, viva, end term.

    On the Convergence of StudentsIntroduction

    1Introduction

    MLF, MLT and MLP are three different kinds of course that happen to share the letters ML. MLF is a mathematics course: no model is trained, and nearly every question is a calculation you do by hand. MLT is a theory course about algorithms: you learn what each model assumes, what it minimises and how it is trained, and the exams test whether you can reason about that on paper. MLP is a programming course: you do the same things in Python with scikit-learn, and it is assessed through code and numbers, not derivations [1, 2, 3].

    The MLP Project is different again. It runs as a real Kaggle competition among students. You must beat a score cutoff, submit only from one named notebook, and then defend your work in two vivas with an industry examiner [4, 6].

    Most of the confusion around these courses comes from treating them as one subject. They reward different habits. MLF rewards solving problems by hand every single week. MLT rewards being able to derive a method and explain why it works. MLP rewards clean code that produces exactly the number the grader expects. Section 2 shows how the courses depend on each other, Sections 4–7 take each course in turn, and Section 8 gives a method for every kind of assessment.

    How to read this.If you are taking MLF and MLT together, which is the usual first machine learning term, read Sections 2–5 and 8. If you are taking MLP, read Sections 6 and 8. If you have the project, Section 7 is written for you. The marks calculator is in Section 9, the term calendar in Section 10, every resource in Section 11, and short answers to common doubts in Appendix A.

    Remark 1.1 (Sep 2026 term). Course registration for the Sep 2026 term runs on 22 and 23 September 2026, the term starts on Friday 2 October, and the end term is on Sunday 10 January 2027 [7]. The course-level details for Sep 2026 (assignment deadlines, Kaggle assignment and project dates) come out in that term's grading document. Until then this guide quotes the May 2026 document [5], and says so wherever a date or a weight is involved.

    4Machine Learning Foundations (MLF)

    MLF is taught by Harish Guruprasad Ramaswamy, Arun Rajkumar and Prashanth L.A. The course page describes it honestly: it “lays the groundwork for the upcoming ML courses by covering various fundamentals that do not necessarily fall under Machine Learning but are quite necessary for a comprehensive understanding of Machine Learning” [1].     4.2What makes it hard

    The linear algebra block, Weeks 3 to 6, is the steepest stretch. It moves fast, it builds on itself, and Week 5's complex and Hermitian matrices feel far from machine learning until you see that they are the reason PCA and the SVD work. A doubt left over from Week 3 becomes three doubts by Week 6.

    The optimisation weeks are where MLF turns into machine learning. Gradient descent, convexity, Lagrange multipliers and the KKT conditions are exactly the tools MLT uses for regression, logistic regression and support vector machines, so time spent here pays twice.

    Almost every question is numerical. In a numeric-entry question a slip in the last line costs the whole question, which makes checking your arithmetic a skill worth practising on its own.

    4.3A week that works

    Algorithm 1: One MLF week

    1. for each week w do
    2. watch the lectures of w with a notebook open; pause at every worked example and redo it before the lecturer does
    3. attempt the practice assignment without notes
    4. for each question you got wrong do
    5. find the minute of the lecture that explains it, and write the idea in one line
    6. end for
    7. attempt the graded assignment; check each numeric answer a second way e.g. verify an eigenvector by computing Av
    8. write the week on one page: definitions, results, one example
    9. end for
    10. before each quiz, redo the assignments of the weeks in scope, then past papers against the clock Quiz 1 covers Weeks 1–4, Quiz 2 covers Weeks 1–8 [8]

    Books that match the course.Chapters 2 to 7 of Mathematics for Machine Learning [22] (linear algebra, geometry, matrix decompositions, vector calculus, probability, optimisation) follow MLF almost chapter for chapter, and the book is free. For convexity, duality and KKT, Boyd and Vandenberghe [23] is the standard text, also free. The subspace and projection weeks are Strang's approach, so his lectures [30] are the natural companion.

    4.4How MLF is marked

    The May 2026 grading document gives the total course score T as [5]

    T = 0.05 GAA + max( 0.6 F + 0.25 max(Qz1, Qz2), 0.4 F + 0.25 Qz1 + 0.3 Qz2 )
    (1)
    whereGAA is the average of the best 10 of your 11 scores for the Week 1–10 graded assignments and Mock Quiz 1,
    Qz1, Qz2 are the quiz scores (0 if you did not attend), and F is the end term score, all out of 100.

    The grader takes the better of two formulas. The first counts only your better quiz, so a missed or bad quiz does not sink you; the second rewards doing well in both, and gives Quiz 2 the larger weight. You never have to choose: the maximum is taken for you.

    A worked example.With GAA = 80, Qz1 = 50, Qz2 = 70 and F = 60, the first formula gives 36 + 17.5 = 53.5 and the second gives 24 + 12.5 + 21 = 57.5. So T = 4 + 57.5 = 61.5. Try your own numbers in Section 9.

    Proposition 2 (End term eligibility, MLF and MLT). You may sit the end term only if the average of your best 5 of 8 scores (the Week 1–7 graded assignments and Mock Quiz 1) is at least 40, and you attended at least one of the two quizzes. To receive a grade at all, you must then sit the end term.

    Proof. Grading document [5], MLF and MLT sections, as updated on 15 July 2026. Mock Quiz 1 appears on your dashboard as “Mock Assignment”.

    Trap.The Week 11 and 12 assignments are not part of GAA, so it is tempting to skip them. Their content is in the end term, and the grading document says so in as many words [5]. Do them.

    5.3Books and notes

    The course page prescribes three books: Duda, Hart and Stork's Pattern Classification [27], Bishop's Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning [24], and Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman's The Elements of Statistical Learning [25] [2]. The last two are free from their authors. Bishop lines up well with the course: linear regression is his Chapter 3, linear classifiers Chapter 4, neural networks Chapter 5, SVMs Chapter 7, mixtures and EM Chapter 9, PCA Chapter 12 and boosting Chapter 14.

    If those feel heavy, An Introduction to Statistical Learning [26] is the gentle version of ESL, and Stanford's CS229 notes [28] derive most of the same algorithms in a few pages each. Closest of all to the course are the notes written for it: the MLT notes on the programme's GitHub [19] and a second set of weekly MLT notes [20].

    5.4How MLT is marked

    MLT uses the same formula as MLF [5]:

    T = 0.05 GAA + max( 0.6 F + 0.25 max(Qz1, Qz2), 0.4 F + 0.25 Qz1 + 0.3 Qz2 )
    (2)

    with the same GAA (best 10 of Weeks 1–10 and Mock Quiz 1) and the same end term eligibility as Proposition 2. MLT adds one thing: a bonus of 3 marks for submitting the programming assignments, if the average of all your assignments is at least 40 [5]. Like every bonus in the programme, it is added only if you pass, and it can lift your grade but not turn a fail into a pass.

    Remark 5.1 (live sessions). The MLT course team runs its own YouTube channel with TA sessions for each week, instructor sessions, workshops and a past-paper session every term [13].

    6.2How MLP is marked

    MLP has no quizzes. Its score comes from weekly assignments, two online remote-proctored programming exams (OPPE 1 and OPPE 2), three Kaggle assignments (KA) and the in-person end term [5]:

    T = 0.1 GAA + 0.3 F + 0.2 OPPE1 + 0.2 OPPE2 + 0.2 KA
    (3)
    whereGAA is the best 10 of Weeks 1–10 and Mock Quiz 1, as in MLF, and KA is the average of the three Kaggle assignments.

    Read the weights once more: 60% of MLP is the two OPPEs and the Kaggle assignments, all of them code. Only 30% is the end term. The course is a programming course in the way it is marked, not only in what it teaches.

    Proposition 3 (Getting an MLP grade). You may sit the MLP end term if the average of your best 5 of 8 scores (Weeks 1–7 and Mock Quiz 1) is at least 40; no quiz is needed. To receive a grade you must then sit the end term and score at least 40/100 in at least one of the two OPPEs. OPPE 1 is scheduled only if you complete the System Compatibility Test (SCT) first.

    Proof. Grading document [5], MLP section and the OPPE eligibility rules.

    6.3The Kaggle assignments

    Each KA is a small Kaggle competition with a notebook you submit and a peer review after it. In May 2026 they ran 10–22 July, 27 July–4 August and 10–18 August, each with a peer-review deadline a few days later [5]. Together they are 20% of the course, as much as either OPPE.

    What I learned doing them.Three habits mattered more than the choice of model. First, optimise the metric you are scored on: when a task is scored by F1, the probability threshold you cut at moves the score more than switching models does, and 0.5 is rarely the right cut. Second, distrust a perfect validation score: in one assignment, random cross-validation gave nearly every model a perfect score, yet the test rows came from whole groups that never appear in training. A split that held out entire groups showed the real spread between models. Third, keep the notebook boring: give it the exact title the instructions ask for (its URL comes from the title and goes into the submission form, so never rename it afterwards), and make sure it always writes submission.csv.

    On the Convergence of StudentsThe MLP Project

    7The MLP Project

    The project is a separate two-credit course, run as a Kaggle competition for 12 to 15 weeks and split into milestones [4]. Every student works alone on the same problem, a regression or a classification task that changes every term. You need to beat a leaderboard cutoff, and then pass two vivas with an industry examiner. Everything below is from the May 2026 guideline [6]; read your own term's guideline the day it is released, because the cutoff, the dates and the notebook name change.

    7.3Milestones, cutoff and marks

    There are five milestones, each a form on the course portal; you must submit at least two, and a milestone counts only once the response copy reaches your email. In May 2026 they fell on 21 June, 28 June, 5 July, 12 July and 21 July, the competition closed on 29 July, and students who did the MLP course that term had until 23 August to finish both vivas. The leaderboard cutoff that term was a score below 0.20 [6]. The final mark out of 100 is

    Final = NB + M + L + S + V
    (4)
    whereNB (10) is your notebook, marked by the examiner for EDA, model training and selection, and model comparison;
    M (5) is for completing all milestones; L (30) scales your leaderboard position; S (5) scales your number of submissions; V (50) is the Level 2 viva.

    Proposition 4 (Passing the project). You pass if the viva score V is at least 25 out of 50 and the final mark is at least 50 out of 100. A viva score between 15 and 24 may be passed with a D or E grade at the instructor's discretion; below 15, the project must be repeated next term.

    Proof. Project guideline, May 2026 [6]. Students who did the MLP course in Jan or May 2026 were marked out of 105, with up to 5 bonus marks by viva batch: Batch 1 earned 5 and a reattempt, Batch 2 earned 2 and a reattempt, Batch 3 earned neither.

    Remark 7.1. The batch rule rewards finishing early. Crossing the cutoff in the first weeks gets you into Batch 1, which is worth 5 marks and a second chance at the viva.

    7.4The two vivas

    Level 1is a checklist, not a score. The examiner checks that you understood the problem and loaded the data correctly; that your EDA covers single features and pairs of features with readable plots; that missing values, scaling and encoding are handled with reusable pipelines or functions applied identically to train and test; that you engineered features; that you tried at least three models from different families, tuned them, and compared them with suitable metrics; and that the notebook is clean and contains your best model [6].

    Level 2decides your marks. It covers conceptual understanding, the mathematics, the practical side and programming. You may be asked to walk through your notebook in five minutes, to explain the five components of any model you used or learned (Section 5.2), and to write code from scratch or change your code on the spot. The examiner can ask about anything in the diploma, including MLF, MLT and statistics, even if the competition was a classification problem and the question is about regression. The guideline puts it bluntly: “Only using an API (e.g. DecisionTreeClassifier, SVC, etc.) is not sufficient, you should also know how it works internally” [6].

    7.5What worked for me

    None of this is code or a solution, and the competition changes each term. These are the habits that moved my score, in the order I would try them again.

    1. Train on the metric. My term's competition was scored on a log error, so I trained on the logarithm of the target. That one change made the training loss equal the metric and tamed a skewed target.
    2. Encode big categories carefully. For columns with thousands of categories, target encoding works only if it is computed out of fold (a row's own label never feeds its own value) and smoothed towards the overall mean; without that, it leaks. Letting LightGBM or CatBoost handle the categories natively turned out just as good, and simpler.
    3. Average, don't over-tune. Averaging the same few models over several random seeds improved my real leaderboard score. A long hyperparameter search looked better in my cross-validation and then scored worse on the leaderboard.
    4. Believe the leaderboard over your laptop. When local validation and the leaderboard disagreed, the leaderboard was right, including about pseudo-labelling, which helped locally and hurt on the leaderboard.
    5. Write submission.csv early. Long notebooks time out. Write the file after each stage so a timeout still leaves a valid submission.
