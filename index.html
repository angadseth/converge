01

The three courses

MLF, MLT and MLP share the letters ML and almost nothing else. One is maths, one is theory, one is code. They reward different habits, and most of the pain in these courses comes from studying all three the same way.

The project is a separate two-credit course with its own registration. Its fee stays valid for two terms.

Together with the MLP Project they make up most of the Diploma in Data Science. Here is what each one is, straight from the official course pages and the grading document.

Who wrote this

I took these courses in this programme: an S grade in MLT with 100/100 in the end term, and 11th of 2,593 on the Kaggle leaderboard of the MLP course's competition. Everything below is either from an official page, linked where it is used, or clearly marked as my own experience.

Which order to take them

There is only one hard rule: MLT before MLP. Everything else can share a term.

Rule

MLF is a co-requisite of MLT: take it before MLT or in the same term. MLT is a prerequisite of MLP: finish it first. MLP is a co-requisite of the MLP Project and of Tools in Data Science.

Two things nobody tells you

Week 4 of MLF is eigenvalues. Watch it in your first week of MLT.

MLF and MLT together, the most common pairing, starts out of sync. MLT opens with PCA in Week 1. The MLF lectures only reach eigenvalues in Week 4 and PCA in Week 7. For about a month MLT uses maths that MLF has not taught you yet. Watch the MLF eigenvalue lectures early, or 3Blue1Brown's short chapter on eigenvectors, and the gap closes.

MLP and its project in the same term is allowed, but the guideline warns against it: "If you are new to ML, you should complete the MLP course in one term and the MLP project in a later term." The project's milestones don't follow the course's week order, so you would be learning some topics before the lectures reach them.

04

MLF: Machine Learning Foundations

A maths course wearing an ML name tag. In the course page's own words, it covers "fundamentals that do not necessarily fall under Machine Learning but are quite necessary" for it.

93 lectures, about 37 hours. Every one is on YouTube.

Calculus, linear algebra, optimisation and probability, each tied back to a machine learning problem. Taught by Harish Guruprasad Ramaswamy, Arun Rajkumar and Prashanth L.A. Here is the course week by week, from the titles in the official lecture playlist. The course page orders the middle weeks a little differently; follow your portal.

What makes it hard

Weeks 3 to 6, the linear algebra block. It moves fast and builds on itself: a doubt left over from Week 3 becomes three doubts by Week 6. Week 5's Hermitian and unitary matrices feel far from ML until you see they are the reason PCA and the SVD work.

The optimisation weeks are where it turns into ML. Gradient descent, convexity, Lagrange multipliers and KKT are exactly the tools MLT uses for regression, logistic regression and SVMs. Time spent here pays twice.

Almost every question is a number. One slip in the last line costs the whole question, so checking your arithmetic is a skill worth practising on its own.

A week that works

  1. Watch with a notebook open. Pause at every worked example and try it before the lecturer finishes it.
  2. Do the practice assignment without notes. Then, for every miss, find the minute of the lecture that explains it.
  3. Do the graded assignment, and check each number a second way. For example, verify an eigenvector by actually computing Av.
  4. Write the week on one page: definitions, results, one example. Twelve pages is your revision for the end term.
  5. Before each quiz, redo the assignments in scope and then past papers against the clock. Quiz 1 covers Weeks 1–4, Quiz 2 covers Weeks 1–8.

Free book that matches the course: Mathematics for Machine Learning, chapters 2 to 7.

Books that match. Chapters 2–7 of Mathematics for Machine Learning follow MLF almost chapter for chapter, and it is free. For convexity and KKT, Boyd and Vandenberghe is the standard text, also free. The subspace weeks are Gilbert Strang's own framing, so his MIT course is the natural companion.

How MLF is marked

T = 0.05·GAA + max( 0.6·F + 0.25·max(Qz1, Qz2), 0.4·F + 0.25·Qz1 + 0.3·Qz2 )

GAA is the average of your best 10 of 11 scores (Week 1–10 assignments and Mock Quiz 1). Qz1, Qz2 are the quiz scores, 0 if you missed one. F is the end term. All out of 100. From the May 2026 grading document.

The grader takes the better of two formulas. The first counts only your better quiz, so one bad or missed quiz does not sink you. The second rewards doing well in both and gives Quiz 2 more weight. You never choose; the max is taken for you.

Worked example. GAA 80, Quiz 1 50, Quiz 2 70, end term 60. First formula: 36 + 17.5 = 53.5. Second: 24 + 12.5 + 21 = 57.5. So T = 4 + 57.5 = 61.5. Try your own numbers in the calculator.

Rule

To sit the end term: the average of your best 5 of 8 scores (Week 1–7 assignments and Mock Quiz 1) must be at least 40, and you must attend at least one of the two quizzes. The same rule applies to MLT.

Source: grading document, MLF and MLT sections, updated 15 July 2026. Mock Quiz 1 shows up on your dashboard as "Mock Assignment".

Trap: skipping Weeks 11 and 12

Their assignments are not part of GAA, so it is tempting to skip them. Their content is in the end term, and the grading document says so in as many words. Do them.

The one habit: five components for every model

For every method, be able to say five things: what data it expects, what model it can represent, what loss it minimises, how it is trained, and how it is evaluated. This is not my framing. It is exactly what the MLP Project's Level 2 viva asks for, for "every machine learning model that you have learnt and/or used", and the programme even has a short official series on it called MLT to MLP. Books and notes

Bishop's chapter map: regression 3, classifiers 4, neural nets 5, SVMs 7, EM 9, PCA 12, boosting 14.

The course page prescribes three books: Duda, Hart and Stork's Pattern Classification, Bishop's Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning, and The Elements of Statistical Learning. The last two are free from their authors. If they feel heavy, An Introduction to Statistical Learning is the gentle version, and Stanford's CS229 notes derive most of the same algorithms in a few pages each. Closest of all are notes written for this course: the MLT notes on the programme's GitHub, with slides and Colab notebooks for each week.

How MLT is marked

T = 0.05·GAA + max( 0.6·F + 0.25·max(Qz1, Qz2), 0.4·F + 0.25·Qz1 + 0.3·Qz2 )

The same formula, GAA and eligibility as MLF. Plus a bonus of 3 marks for submitting the programming assignments, if your average across all assignments is at least 40.

Like every bonus in the programme, it is added only if you pass. It can lift your grade; it cannot turn a fail into a pass.

The MLT course team also runs its own YouTube channel with weekly TA sessions, instructor sessions, workshops and a past-paper session every term.

The Kaggle assignments

Each KA is a small Kaggle competition with a notebook you submit and a peer review after it. In May 2026 they ran 10–22 July, 27 July–4 August and 10–18 August. Together they are 20% of the course, as much as either OPPE.

From my own May 2026 assignments. Approach only; the datasets change.

Three habits mattered more than the choice of model.

  1. Optimise the metric you are scored on. When a task is scored by F1, the probability threshold you cut at moves the score more than switching models does, and 0.5 is rarely the right cut.
  2. Distrust a perfect validation score. In one assignment, random cross-validation gave nearly every model a perfect score, yet the test rows came from whole groups that never appear in training. A split that held out entire groups showed the real spread.
  3. Keep the notebook boring. Give it the exact title the instructions ask for (its URL comes from the title and goes into the submission form, so never rename it afterwards), and make sure it always writes submission.csv.
07

The MLP Project

A real Kaggle competition, 12 to 15 weeks long, where everyone works alone on the same problem. Beat the cutoff, then pass two vivas with an industry examiner.

Everything here is from the May 2026 guideline. Read your own term's the day it comes out: the cutoff, the dates and the notebook name change.

The problem is a regression or a classification task, new every term. Rules below are from the MLP Project Guideline for May 2026.

Register exactly like this

Most project trouble in the first weeks is registration trouble. The guideline's steps, in order:

  1. Make a Kaggle account and join the competition from the link in the guideline.
  2. Create a notebook in the competition's Code tab named rollno-notebook-term, all lowercase. May 2026's example: 21f1001234-notebook-2026t2.
  3. Share it with the Kaggle user iitmbscs2008p (view access is enough) and keep it private.
  4. Make a first submission from that notebook. A dummy model is fine.
  5. Paste the notebook link into the registration form before the deadline. Late registration costs 1 mark a week.
  6. Check the registration status sheet. It updates every 72 hours.
  7. Never rename that notebook, and never submit from any other.

Milestones, cutoff and marks

Five milestones, each a form on the course portal. You must submit at least two, and one only counts once the response copy reaches your email. In May 2026 they fell on 21 June, 28 June, 5 July, 12 July and 21 July. The competition closed on 29 July, the cutoff was a leaderboard score below 0.20, and both vivas had to be done by 23 August.

Final = NB + M + L + S + V   (out of 10 + 5 + 30 + 5 + 50 = 100)

NB your notebook, marked by the examiner. M all milestones done. L your leaderboard position. S how many submissions you made. V the Level 2 viva.

Rule

You pass if your viva is at least 25/50 and your final mark is at least 50/100. A viva of 15–24 may still pass with a D or E at the instructor's discretion. Below 15, you repeat the project next term.

Source: MLP Project Guideline, May 2026. Jan and May 2026 MLP students were marked out of 105: up to 5 bonus marks by viva batch (Batch 1: +5 and a reattempt, Batch 2: +2 and a reattempt, Batch 3: none).

The bonus goes to the earlier viva batches, so being ready early pays. Check your own term's guideline for how batches are assigned.

The two vivas

Level 1 is a checklist, not a score. The examiner checks that you understood the problem and loaded the data right; that your EDA looks at single features and pairs of features with readable plots; that missing values, scaling and encoding are handled with reusable pipelines applied the same way to train and test; that you engineered features; that you tried at least three models from different families, tuned them and compared them with suitable metrics; and that the notebook is clean and holds your best model.

Level 2 decides your marks. Concepts, maths, practice and programming. You may be asked to walk through your notebook in five minutes, to explain the five components of any model you used or learned, or to write code on the spot. The examiner can ask about anything in the diploma, MLF, MLT and statistics included. In the guideline's words: "Only using an API (e.g. DecisionTreeClassifier, SVC, etc.) is not sufficient, you should also know how it works internally."

What worked for me

No code, no solution. The competition is different every term.

  1. Train on the metric. My term's competition was scored on a log error, so I trained on the logarithm of the target. The training loss became the metric, and a skewed target calmed down.
  2. Encode big categories carefully. Target encoding only works for columns with thousands of categories if it is computed out of fold and smoothed towards the overall mean; otherwise it leaks. Letting LightGBM or CatBoost handle categories natively was just as good, and simpler.
  3. Average, don't over-tune. Averaging the same few models over several random seeds improved my real leaderboard score. A long hyperparameter search looked better in cross-validation and then scored worse.
  4. Believe the leaderboard over your laptop. When they disagreed, the leaderboard was right, including about pseudo-labelling, which helped locally and hurt on Kaggle.
  5. Write submission.csv early. Long notebooks time out. Write the file after each stage, so a timeout still leaves a valid submission.
  6. Rehearse the viva as code. I prepared a five-minute walkthrough and practised writing PCA, a pipeline, target encoding, k-means and ridge regression without notes.
08

Every assessment, and how to take it

Weekly assignments carry little weight, but Weeks 1 to 7 decide whether you may sit the end term at all. Treat the early weeks as the ones that matter most.

Weekly graded assignments

Every week of every course has one, with a fixed deadline in the grading document; the May 2026 document says, in capitals, that none of them will move. They are 5% of MLF and MLT and 10% of MLP.

In MLF and MLT you get multiple choice, multiple select and numeric answers. Treat each option of a multiple-select question as its own true-or-false question. In a numeric one, read the rounding instruction first, keep full precision through the working, and round once, at the end. When a definition has two versions (dividing a variance by n or by n−1), use the one the lectures use.

In MLP most questions hand you a dataset and a precise recipe and ask for a number: train this model with these settings, report this metric on this split, to three decimals. The marks go to the exact number, so the job is matching the recipe, not modelling well:

  1. Load the data exactly as asked: same file, same columns, same split and random_state.
  2. Set every parameter the question names; leave the rest at scikit-learn's defaults.
  3. Fit on the training set only, then apply the fitted transformers and model to the test set.
  4. Compute the metric asked for, on the set asked for, and round only at the end.
  5. If no option matches, re-read the split and the parameters, then check your scikit-learn version: defaults change between versions.

Am I eligible for the end term?

Type your Week 1–7 scores and Mock Quiz 1. It takes the best 5 of the 8. Leave a box empty if that assignment isn't out yet; it counts as zero. Nothing leaves your browser.

Quizzes (MLF and MLT)

Written in person at an exam centre. Quiz 1 covers Weeks 1–4 and Quiz 2 covers Weeks 1–8. It is one sitting for all your quiz courses: 2 hours for one or two courses, 3 for three, 4 for four. There is no make-up quiz, and a missed quiz scores zero (Academics page). In the Sep 2026 term, Quiz 1 is on Sunday 15 November and Quiz 2 on Saturday 5 December.

Mock Quiz 1 counts: it is in both GAA and the eligibility check.

Each course runs a mock before Quiz 1, and it counts. Where a course runs mocks before both quizzes, an average of 40 or more across them can earn up to 2 bonus marks. Past papers are the best preparation there is; chapter 10 lists students who have solved them on video.

OPPEs (MLP)

Online, remote-proctored programming exams, on your own computer. Pass the System Compatibility Test first, or OPPE 1 is not scheduled at all. In the Sep 2026 term OPPE 1 runs on 21–22 November, and OPPE 2 on 19–20 December and 2–3 January; your own slot is allotted closer to the date.

The practice that transfers best: redo the weekly MLP assignments against a clock until loading, splitting, fitting and scoring is muscle memory. A student-made OPPE 1 mock on Kaggle shows you the format. Read the exam's own instructions for what may be open on your screen; don't assume.

The end term

In person; in the Sep 2026 term, Sunday 10 January 2027. It covers all twelve weeks, including Weeks 11 and 12. It is 40–60% of MLF and MLT depending on which half of the formula wins, and 30% of MLP. In MLF and MLT a strong end term can rescue a weak quiz. In MLP no end term score can stand in for passing an OPPE.
